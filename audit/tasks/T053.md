## Conclusion – structure (prose, no lists; about the current length plus at most one third of a page)

1. **Opening:** one sentence that returns to the question (did developers target the red lines, and what does compliance consist of?).
2. **Results, weighted:** primary (concentration at RL1: excess mass rises by 4.02), secondary (mechanical role of minority interests: 11.5 percentage points; growth did not exceed its pre-policy pace and, under the firm trend, was slower), exploratory (public filings classify the terms of only 10 of 249 equity transactions). One interval at most, in the primary result.
3. **Policy implications (new, carefully worded, derived from the findings):**
   (a) *Disclosure:* because public filings rarely reveal the terms of minority stakes, supervisors could require developers to report, for material non-wholly-owned subsidiaries, the counterparty, buyback or put obligations, guarantees and shareholder loans – the information that our coding needed and seldom found.
   (b) *Look-through measurement:* a ratio could treat minority equity with buyback, guaranteed-return or shortfall terms as debt, so that compliance reflects economic leverage more closely.
   Phrase both as implications that follow if the aim is to measure risk ("our findings suggest", "would allow"), not as proven effects; say that their costs and feasibility are outside this proposal.
4. **Transferability:** one or two sentences that any regulation tied to a reported ratio – loan covenants, bank capital ratios – faces the same question of which adjustments count, linking to the threshold-response literature already cited in Section 2 (use existing keys only, e.g. Dichev and Skinner 2002, Owens et al. 2015).
5. **Limits:** keep the current content in two or three sentences (no causal policy effect; economic leverage not observed; missing terms are not evidence of debt-like equity).
6. **Plan:** shorten to two sentences and refer to Section 6.5 for details; no repetition.
