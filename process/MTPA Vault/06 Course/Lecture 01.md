## [number withheld] Policy analysis vs. academic research (slides [number withheld]–[number withheld])

| Traditional research | Policy analysis |
|---|---|
| Seeks "truth" | Is practical |
| Explicit steps and procedures | Flexible, situational |
| Broad questions | Local problems |
| Focus on complexity | Focus on decision-making |
| Science | Craft |

The difference "should not be overemphasized" (slide [number withheld]). The two form a positive cycle: research provides the foundation for evidence-based policy; implementation reveals gaps that generate new research (examples on slide [number withheld]: tobacco taxation; Targeted Poverty Alleviation in China).

## [number withheld] Introductory example: the kidney shortage (slides [number withheld]–[number withheld])

A model policy memo to the NHC minister, adapted from Weimer & Vining ([number withheld]), ch. [number withheld]. The full case is summarised in [[Weekly Reading - Week [number withheld]]]. Key writing and analysis lessons:

- **Write to your client.** Answer the question asked, on time, in an easy-to-follow form (slide [number withheld]). The "Concerning" line is a problem statement, not just a topic (slide [number withheld]).
- **Executive summary features** (slides [number withheld]–[number withheld]): ([number withheld]) brevity; ([number withheld]) state the issue and the recommendation as early as possible; ([number withheld]) rehearse the complete analysis and the reason for the recommendation. Always include **current policy** as an alternative. State the **evaluation criteria before assessing alternatives** (prevents cherry-picking).
- **From section [number withheld] onward, every statement needs data or evidence. "Be responsible for what you say!"** (slide [number withheld]).
- **Two tasks before analysis** (slide [number withheld]): ([number withheld]) *diagnose* the undesirable condition, quantitatively if possible ("What is wrong?"); ([number withheld]) *frame* it as a market failure, a government failure or both ("Why is it wrong?"). Without framing we risk treating symptoms instead of causes (traffic example, slide [number withheld]). For the kidney case the cause is both: an unregulated market is unacceptable, and the market ban is questionable (slide [number withheld]).
- **Policy goals** (slides [number withheld]–[number withheld]): be explicit about values. Human dignity, equity, efficiency, fiscal impact and political feasibility; quantify criteria where possible (e.g. [number withheld] QALYs per transplant).
- **Alternatives** (slides [number withheld]–[number withheld]): status quo plus "borrowing and tinkering" with existing proposals; specify enough detail to predict impacts.
- **Assessment** (slides [number withheld]–[number withheld]): the "modern tools" part. Draw on existing research, "squeeze" information creatively, and expose assumptions. The slides critique the reading itself: evidence on donations to *strangers* is used to predict donations to *relatives* (slide [number withheld]). Policy analysts often must make out-of-sample predictions that academics avoid (slide [number withheld]).
- **Trade-offs and recommendation** (slides [number withheld]–[number withheld]): no alternative dominates on all goals. Recommend one policy explicitly. A summary comparison table works as a decision aid (Appendix A).
- **Transparency about limitations** in policy memos (slide [number withheld]), which "we don't usually talk about in advance in academic research".
