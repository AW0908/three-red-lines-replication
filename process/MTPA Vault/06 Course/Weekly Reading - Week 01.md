## [number withheld]. Policy Analysis in Perspective

A one-page table adapted from Weimer & Vining ([number withheld]), Table [number withheld], comparing six paradigms:

| Paradigm | Major objective | "Client" | Common style | Time constraints | General weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical planning | Define and achieve a desirable future state of society | "Public interest" as professionally defined | Established rules and norms; goals and objectives | Little immediate pressure (long-term future) | Wishful thinking when political processes are ignored |
| "Old" public administration | Efficiently execute programs set by political processes | "Public interest" embodied in the mandated program | Managerial and legal | Tied to routine decisions such as budget cycles | Excludes alternatives external to the program |
| Journalism | Focus public attention on societal problems | General public | Descriptive | Strong deadline pressure | Lack of analytical depth and balance |
| **Policy analysis** | Systematically compare and evaluate alternatives available to public actors | Specific person or institution as decision maker | Synthesis of existing research and theory to predict consequences of alternatives | Strong deadline pressure, tied to a specific decision | Myopia from client orientation and time pressure |
| **Policy research** | Evaluate impacts of changes in variables that public policy can alter | Actors in the policy arena; related disciplines | Formal methodology applied to policy-relevant questions; prediction of consequences | Sometimes deadline pressure, mitigated by issue recurrence | Difficulty translating findings into government action |
| Academic social science research | Construct theories for understanding society | "Truth" as defined by the disciplines; other scholars | Rigorous methods for constructing and testing theories; usually retrospective | Rarely external time constraints | Often irrelevant to decision makers' information needs |

## Takeaways

- [ ] **Lead with the question and the answer.** The syllabus requires question, method, motivation and contribution within the first three introduction paragraphs ([[Proposal Requirements]]). This mirrors the memo's "recommendation early" principle.
- [ ] **State criteria and alternatives explicitly**, including the status quo. For our project: the counterfactual of no policy, and the comparison of reported vs. economic leverage ([[Research Question]]).
- [ ] **Every factual claim needs a source, date and units.** Unsourced or placeholder evidence is an explicit "Avoid" (Safeguards).
- [ ] **Match the evidence to the mechanism and population.** Do not use evidence on one population (strangers / leverage effects) to claim another (relatives / off-balance-sheet shifting) (Literature Gap).
- [ ] **Show arithmetic and assumptions; give ranges, not false precision.** Relevant for adjusted leverage and JV debt proxies (Empirical Design, Variables and Metrics).
- [ ] **Position the project.** Our proposal is *policy research* in the table's sense: it evaluates the impact of a policy-alterable variable (the red-line thresholds) with formal methods. Its typical weakness, difficulty translating findings into action, could be addressed by a short policy-implication paragraph.
